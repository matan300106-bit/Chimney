Chimneys in Murrysville

Room to spread out, and wood to burn

Murrysville sits just east of Monroeville in Westmoreland County, with Route 22 running through it. It has a more open, wooded feel than the suburbs closer to the city: larger lots, winding roads, older farmhouses, and subdivisions built over the last several decades.

With space to store firewood and plenty of trees around, many homeowners here heat with wood all winter. That means freestanding wood stoves, fireplace inserts and well-used open fireplaces.

A stove or insert that runs every day all winter can build creosote quickly, especially with damp wood or a fire turned down low overnight. Older farmhouse chimneys may have been built long before modern liners existed. And wooded lots bring animals: raccoons, squirrels and birds all like an open flue.

If you heat with wood

Know your creosote

Creosote is what's left when smoke cools in the flue. It builds in stages, and each one is harder to remove and more dangerous than the last.

Stage 1

Soot and flakes

Dry, powdery and easy to brush out. A regular sweep handles it.

Stage 2

Crunchy and tarry

Shiny, brittle flakes. Harder to remove and a sign the flue is running cool.

Stage 3

Glazed

A hard, tar-like coating that's very flammable. Needs specialized removal, and sometimes a liner afterward.

How often should a wood stove chimney be swept?
If you heat with wood, have it inspected every year and swept whenever creosote builds up. For a stove running daily all winter that's at least once a year, and some households need a mid-season check too. A common rule of thumb is to sweep once there's about an eighth of an inch of buildup.
There's chirping in my chimney in summer. What is it?
It's often chimney swifts, a migratory bird protected by federal law. Active nests can't legally be removed. We can confirm what's there, and once the birds leave for the season we'll clean the flue and fit a cap so they nest elsewhere next year.
Our farmhouse chimney has no liner. Can we use it for a wood stove?
Not until it's been inspected. Older unlined chimneys often have gaps and deteriorated mortar inside. A stainless liner sized to the stove is the usual fix and gives the exhaust a safe, smooth path out.
