How often should you sweep your chimney?

Have it inspected once a year, and swept whenever there's about 1/8 inch of sooty buildup or any glazed creosote. For most Pittsburgh homes that burn wood regularly, that means a sweep every year.

The short answer

Two pieces of national guidance shape how often a chimney should be cleaned. The first is NFPA 211, the fire-safety standard for chimneys, fireplaces and vents. It says chimneys should be inspected at least once a year and cleaned, maintained or repaired as needed. The second is the rule of thumb used by the Chimney Safety Institute of America (CSIA): sweep when there's about 1/8 inch of sooty buildup inside the flue, and sooner if there's any glazed creosote.

Put together, the honest answer is "inspect every year, sweep when the inspection says so." In practice, a wood-burning fireplace or stove that gets used through a Pittsburgh winter often reaches that 1/8-inch mark within a season, so an annual sweep is the norm. A gas fireplace may go years without needing a brush, but it still needs that yearly look.

How often, by fuel type

What burns in your appliance decides what builds up in the flue, and how fast. One thing doesn't change: every one of these should be inspected once a year. Here's how the sweeping side works out for the systems we see most in Greater Pittsburgh.

ApplianceWhen to sweepWhat builds up
Wood fireplaceAt 1/8" of buildup. Usually yearly with regular useSoot and creosote
Wood stove or insertUsually yearly. Heavy use or damp wood may need a mid-season checkCreosote, often faster than an open fireplace
Pellet stoveYearly, plus the vent cleaning in the owner's manualFly ash and fine soot
Gas fireplace or logsAs neededLittle soot. Nests, debris, vent or liner wear
Oil furnace or boilerOften yearly, alongside burner serviceSoot and scale that can drop and block the base
Gas furnace or water heaterAs neededDebris, nests, tile damage from condensation

Gas and oil flues don't build up creosote the way wood does, but a blocked or damaged flue on a furnace, boiler or water heater can push carbon monoxide back into the house. That's why the yearly check still matters even when you never light a fire.

What makes buildup happen faster

Two homes with identical fireplaces can need very different schedules. Creosote forms when smoke cools and condenses on the flue walls, so anything that makes smoke cooler, wetter or slower adds to it.

  • Unseasoned or wet wood. Burning wet wood spends heat boiling off water and makes a smoky, cool fire. Well-seasoned firewood is generally 20% moisture or less; an inexpensive moisture meter will tell you.
  • Smoldering fires. Turning a stove's air control way down to make a load last overnight is one of the fastest ways to coat a flue.
  • A cold, exterior chimney. Many older Pittsburgh houses have chimneys on an outside wall. The flue stays colder, so more smoke condenses on the way up.
  • An oversized flue. A stove or insert vented into a big old masonry flue without a properly sized liner can draft poorly and build creosote quickly.
  • Heavy use. If wood is a main heat source rather than an occasional weekend fire, plan on more frequent checks.

Signs it can't wait for next year

The calendar is a reminder, not a guarantee. Book a sweep sooner if you notice any of these:

  • A strong campfire or tar smell from the fireplace, especially on humid summer days or when the air conditioning runs
  • Smoke rolling into the room, or fires that are harder to start and keep going
  • Black flakes or crumbs falling into the firebox
  • Buildup you can see on the damper or the smoke shelf with a flashlight
  • Scratching, chirping or debris in the firebox, which usually means an animal or nest
  • It's been more than a year since anyone looked
See a shiny, tar-like coating?Glazed, third-degree creosote is the hardest stage to remove and the most dangerous to burn over. Stop using the fireplace until it's been looked at. Our guide to the three stages of creosote explains why.

What if you never use the fireplace?

An unused fireplace still needs an inspection, just usually not a sweep. A chimney that sits idle can collect nests, leaves and fallen mortar, and water damage keeps going whether or not you light a fire. If the chimney also vents your furnace or water heater, it's in use every day all winter, even if the fireplace beside it is purely decorative.

If you've just moved in and don't know when the chimney was last cleaned, assume it hasn't been. Have it swept and inspected before the first fire.

A sweep and an inspection aren't the same thing

Sweeping removes soot and creosote. An inspection tells you whether the chimney is safe to use. NFPA 211 describes three levels of inspection:

  • Level 1 is a visual check of the readily accessible parts of the chimney, flue and appliance. It's right for yearly maintenance when nothing about your system has changed, and it's included with every chimney sweep we do.
  • Level 2 adds a video scan of the inside of the flue and a look at accessible attic, basement and crawlspace areas. It's called for when a home is sold, after a chimney fire or storm damage, or when you change the system, such as a new stove or a switch in fuel.
  • Level 3 involves opening up concealed areas and is only needed when a serious hidden problem is suspected.

More detail is on our chimney inspection page. If you're buying a house, read why buyers should get a Level 2 inspection.

When to book in Pittsburgh

Most people think about the chimney on the first cold night of fall, which is exactly when everyone else does too. A few timing tips:

  • Spring is underrated. Sweeping right after your last fire removes deposits before humid summer weather makes them smell, and any repairs can be done in good masonry weather.
  • Late summer is the next best time. You'll have the chimney ready before you need it.
  • Fall fills up fast. Once the first cold snap hits, schedules get tight. Booking a few weeks ahead helps.

Can you sweep it yourself?

Some homeowners do, with the right brush and rods for their flue size. The cleaning is the easy part. The value of a professional visit is the inspection: spotting cracked flue tiles, a deteriorating crown, a failing damper or the start of glazed creosote, and documenting it. Getting onto a steep Pittsburgh roof safely is its own problem.

Creosote "sweeping logs" and powders can make some deposits easier to remove, but they aren't a substitute for a sweep and an inspection.

The bottom line

  • Inspect every chimney once a year, whatever it burns. That's the NFPA 211 standard.
  • Sweep at about 1/8 inch of buildup, or right away if you see glazed creosote. For regular wood burning, that usually means every year.
  • Burn dry wood in hot, bright fires to slow buildup down.
  • Don't skip gas and oil flues. They make less mess but carry a carbon monoxide risk if blocked.

Common questions

How often should a wood-burning fireplace be swept?
Have it inspected every year and swept when there's about 1/8 inch of buildup. With regular winter use, most wood-burning fireplaces in the Pittsburgh area reach that point within a season, so a yearly sweep is typical.
Do gas fireplaces need to be swept?
Gas burns much cleaner than wood, so they rarely need a full sweep. They still need a yearly inspection. Nests, debris, misplaced logs and deteriorating vents or liners can all cause venting problems, and a gas appliance that doesn't vent properly can release carbon monoxide.
How long does a chimney sweep take?
A standard sweep with a Level 1 inspection usually takes 45 to 90 minutes. Camera inspections and chimneys with more than one flue take longer.
Is it safe to use my fireplace if it hasn't been swept this year?
If you don't know when it was last inspected, it's safest not to use it until someone has looked. Buildup, nests and cracked flue tiles aren't visible from the room. If you notice smoke coming into the house, a strong tar smell or a glossy coating in the flue, stop and have it checked first.
