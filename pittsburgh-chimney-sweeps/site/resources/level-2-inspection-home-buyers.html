Buying a home

Do home buyers need a Level 2 chimney inspection?

Yes, if the house has a chimney you plan to use, and that includes the one venting the furnace or water heater. The national standard, NFPA 211, calls for a Level 2 inspection when a property is sold or transferred, and it's how you see inside the flue before you close.

Why a home inspection isn't enough

A general home inspector looks at the whole house in a few hours: roof, structure, electrical, plumbing, heating and more. For the chimney, that usually means a look at the outside, the firebox and whatever can be seen with a flashlight. Home inspection standards of practice generally don't require the inspector to examine the inside of the flue, and most don't have the camera equipment to do it.

That's not a criticism of home inspectors. It's why many inspection reports say something like "recommend evaluation by a qualified chimney professional." The problems that matter most in a chimney, such as cracked flue tiles, gaps between liner sections, damage from a past chimney fire or a liner that's missing altogether, are inside the flue where you can't see them from the room.

The three inspection levels

NFPA 211 is the national fire-safety standard for chimneys, fireplaces and vents. It defines three levels of inspection, each including everything in the level before it.

LevelWhen it's called forWhat's added
1Yearly maintenance, when nothing about the system has changedVisual check of readily accessible parts of the chimney, flue and appliance
2Sale or transfer of the property, after a chimney fire or other damaging event, or when the system changesVideo scan of the flue interior, plus accessible attic, basement and crawlspace areas
3When a Level 2 points to a serious hidden hazardOpening concealed areas, such as part of a wall or the crown, to get to the problem

"System changes" covers things like a new stove or insert, switching fuel (from oil to gas, for example) or relining the chimney. A Level 3 is rare. It should only be recommended when there's a specific reason to believe something serious is hidden, and the reason should be explained to you.

What turns up in Pittsburgh-area houses

A lot of the housing around Pittsburgh is older, and many chimneys have been working for most of a century. Some were built when coal heat was common and have been adapted several times since. That history shows up in inspections. Common findings include:

  • Cracked or missing clay flue tiles, or gaps where mortar between tile sections has washed out
  • No liner at all. Some older chimneys were built without one.
  • An "orphaned" water heater. When an older furnace is replaced with a high-efficiency model vented through the wall, the water heater can be left venting alone into a flue that's now far too big for it. That can cause poor draft and condensation damage.
  • Signs of a past chimney fire, such as puffy, honeycombed creosote, cracked tiles or a warped damper
  • Gas logs or an insert added to an old fireplace without the venting being checked
  • Crown, cap, flashing and brickwork problems from years of freeze-thaw weather

None of this means you should walk away from a house. It means you'll know what you're buying, and what it may cost to put right.

How to fit it in before closing

Inspection contingency periods are short, so it pays to move fast.

  1. Book as soon as your offer is accepted. Ask for a Level 2 inspection specifically, and give the closing or contingency date when you book.
  2. Coordinate access with the listing agent. We'll need to get to the fireplace, the attic and the basement, and the roof if it's safe to reach.
  3. Ask the seller not to use the fireplace for a day beforehand, so the firebox is cool.
  4. Expect a written report. You'll get photos and findings that both agents can read, with any problems ranked by urgency.
A heavily coated flue may need sweeping firstA camera can't see cracks through thick soot or creosote. If a wood-burning flue is heavily coated, it may need to be swept for the inspection to be complete. We'll tell you when we book if that's likely.

What the report should tell you

A useful Level 2 report answers the questions you'll actually face at the negotiating table:

  • Is each flue safe to use for the appliance connected to it, as it stands today?
  • What's wrong, with photos from inside the flue, not just a description.
  • How urgent each item is: safety problem now, repair soon, or maintenance to plan for.
  • What it takes to fix it, with a written repair estimate. Ours are free.

How you use the findings, whether that's a repair request, a credit or a price change, is between you, your agent and, where needed, your attorney. We can't advise on the deal itself, but we're glad to explain the findings to anyone involved.

A note for sellers

If you're selling, a pre-listing Level 2 inspection removes a surprise from the middle of your sale. You can fix problems on your own schedule, or disclose them up front with a written repair estimate in hand. Agents can find more on our page for realtors.

Questions to ask when you book

Not every "chimney inspection" is a Level 2. Whoever you hire, these questions make sure you get what you need:

  • Is this a Level 2 inspection as defined in NFPA 211?
  • Will every flue be scanned with a camera, including the furnace, boiler and water heater flues?
  • Will I get photos or video from inside the flue with the report?
  • How soon will I have the written report, and can it go straight to my agent?
  • Are your technicians certified? Our inspections are done by CSIA-certified technicians.
  • If you find problems, will I get a written repair estimate?

The bottom line

  • NFPA 211 calls for a Level 2 inspection when a property is sold or transferred.
  • A general home inspection usually doesn't look inside the flue. A Level 2 camera scan does.
  • Include every flue: fireplace, furnace, boiler and water heater.
  • Book it the day your offer is accepted so you have the report inside your contingency window.

Common questions

What's the difference between a Level 1 and a Level 2 chimney inspection?
A Level 1 is a visual check of the readily accessible parts of the chimney, flue and appliance, and is right for yearly maintenance. A Level 2 adds a video scan of the inside of the flue and a look at accessible attic, basement and crawlspace areas. NFPA 211 calls for a Level 2 when a property is sold, after a chimney fire or similar event, and when the system is changed.
Do I need a Level 2 if I never plan to use the fireplace?
If the chimney also vents a furnace, boiler or water heater, yes, because that flue is in use every day. Even for a fireplace you won't use, the inspection tells you about water damage, crumbling masonry or a failing crown that will need attention either way.
How long does a Level 2 inspection take?
It depends on the number of flues and how easy they are to reach. Plan for more time than a standard sweep. We'll give you an estimate when you book.
Who pays for the chimney inspection, the buyer or the seller?
Usually whoever orders it, which is most often the buyer during the inspection period. Some sellers order one before listing. Your agent can tell you what's typical for your deal.
