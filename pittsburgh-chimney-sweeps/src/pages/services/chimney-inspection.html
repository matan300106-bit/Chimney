Why inspect

Most of your chimney is out of sight

From the living room you see a firebox. From the yard you see some brick and a cap. Everything that matters in between, the flue liner, the smoke chamber, the joints between tiles and the framing around the chimney, is hidden. That's where cracks, gaps and creosote do their damage.

A chimney inspection is a systematic look at the whole venting system: the appliance, the connection, the flue, the masonry and the roof penetration. NFPA 211, the national standard for chimneys and vents, calls for one every year, for wood, gas and oil alike.

Pittsburgh gives us extra reasons to look closely. Much of the region's housing is decades old, with clay tile liners that crack and separate with age. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles open up crowns and mortar joints. And many chimneys still vent a gas furnace or water heater, where a blockage or a failing liner is a carbon monoxide risk rather than a fire risk.

When each level applies

NFPA 211 ties the inspection level to what's happening with your home and your chimney. Here's how that works in plain terms. If you're unsure, tell us your situation and we'll recommend the lowest level that answers the question.

Yearly check, nothing has changed, you plan to keep using it the same wayLevel 1A visual check of the readily accessible parts confirms it's sound, clear and safe to keep using.

New furnace, water heater, insert or stove, or switching fuel (wood to gas, oil to gas)Level 2A different appliance may need a different flue size or liner. Changes to the system call for a Level 2.

Signs you need one now

Don't wait for the yearly visit

  • You're under contract on a house, or listing yours
  • A loud roar, popping or heavy smoke from the top of the chimney
  • Flaky, puffy black debris in the firebox after a fire
  • Your CO alarm went off, or anyone has headaches that ease when they leave home
  • You've had a new furnace or water heater installed on the chimney
  • Pieces of tile or mortar falling into the firebox or cleanout
  • A storm, fallen limb or lightning hit near the chimney

Isn't the chimney covered by my home inspection?
Usually only the visible parts. General home inspectors typically look at the firebox, the damper and the outside of the chimney, and they aren't expected to inspect the inside of the flue. A Level 2 chimney inspection includes a camera scan of the full flue, which is where liner cracks and gaps show up.
Do I need a Level 2 if the seller had the chimney swept?
A sweep normally comes with a Level 1 inspection, which only covers readily accessible areas. NFPA 211 calls for a Level 2 at property sale or transfer, so as a buyer you'd want one done for you, with a report you can review before your contingency deadline.
Should the chimney be swept before a camera inspection?
Often, yes. Heavy soot and creosote can hide cracks from the camera. If the flue is dirty we'll tell you, and we can sweep and inspect in the same visit.
My fireplace is gas. Does it still need an inspection?
Yes. NFPA 211 calls for annual inspection of gas venting too. Gas doesn't leave creosote, but nests, debris and deteriorating liners can block the vent and send carbon monoxide back into the house.
What happens in a Level 3 inspection?
It's only used when a Level 1 or 2 points to a hazard we can't evaluate without access to concealed areas. That can mean removing part of a wall, the crown or other components. It's rare. We'll explain exactly why it's needed and what's involved, and get your approval, before anything is opened up.
Will you give me a report I can send to my realtor?
Yes. Every inspection comes with a written photo report you can forward to your agent, the other party or your insurer. If repairs are recommended, the written estimate comes with it.
