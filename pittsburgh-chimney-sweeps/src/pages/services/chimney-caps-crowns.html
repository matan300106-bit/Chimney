Cap, crown, flashing

Three parts people mix up

The cap is the metal cover that sits over the flue opening. Its lid keeps rain and snow out of the flue, and its mesh sides keep out birds, squirrels, raccoons and leaves while letting smoke escape. On a wood-burning chimney the mesh also acts as a spark arrestor.

The crown is the slab of concrete or mortar that covers the top of the brick around the flue tile. A properly built crown slopes away from the flue and overhangs the brick, so water drips clear of the chimney instead of running down its face.

The flashing is the metal that seals the joint between the chimney and the roof. It's a common leak source too, and we cover it on our leaks and waterproofing page.

All three protect the brick below. When any one of them fails, water gets into the masonry, and in a Pittsburgh winter that water freezes, expands and breaks things.

Chimney caps

A cap that fits your chimney

Caps aren't one size fits all. The right one depends on how many flues you have, what's venting through them and how the top of the chimney is built.

Single-flue caps

Fits over one flue tile and fastens to it. The usual choice when each flue sticks up through the crown on its own.

Multi-flue caps

One larger cap that covers the whole top of the chimney, including several flues. It also shelters the crown from direct rain.

Stainless or copper

Stainless steel holds up well and suits most homes. Copper costs more, lasts a long time and weathers to a darker patina. We don't recommend galvanized caps, which rust.

Gas and oil flues need the right cap tooA cap with fine mesh can clog on some appliances and restrict the flue. We match the cap to the appliance that vents through it, and check the mesh every year.
Chimney crowns

Seal it, or rebuild it

Many older chimneys don't have a true crown. They have a thin layer of mortar troweled over the top bricks, sometimes called a mortar wash. It's flat, has no overhang, and cracks quickly as the chimney heats, cools, freezes and thaws.

Hairline cracks in an otherwise sound crown can usually be cleaned and sealed with a flexible crown sealant made for the job. It's a fast repair, and it buys years of protection.

Crumbling, broken or flat crowns are better rebuilt. We remove the old crown down to solid masonry and form a new one that slopes away from the flue, overhangs the brick with a drip edge, and leaves a small gap around the flue tile so the tile can expand and contract without cracking the crown.

Signs your cap or crown needs work

  • No cap at all, or one that's rusted, loose or bent
  • Leaves, twigs or nesting material in the firebox
  • Scratching or chirping in the chimney
  • Water dripping into the firebox, or a rusty damper
  • Visible cracks or chunks missing from the crown
  • Crumbling brick or white staining near the top of the chimney

Cap and crown questions

{{phone}}
Does my chimney really need a cap?
Almost every chimney benefits from one. Without a cap, rain falls straight down the flue, where it rusts dampers, soaks the smoke chamber and damages tile. An open flue is also an easy home for birds, squirrels and raccoons. A cap is one of the least expensive ways to protect a chimney.
What's the difference between a chimney cap and a crown?
The cap is the metal cover over the flue opening. The crown is the concrete or mortar slab that covers the top of the brickwork around the flue. The cap keeps water and animals out of the flue; the crown keeps water out of the brick. You need both.
Should I choose stainless steel or copper?
Both last well. Stainless steel is the practical choice for most homes. Copper costs more and is often chosen for looks, especially on older brick homes, as it weathers to a darker patina. We'll show you both and give you a written price for each.
Can a cap cause draft problems?
A cap that's the wrong size, sits too low, or has mesh clogged with creosote or soot can restrict the flue. That's why we measure each flue, match the cap to the appliance, and check the mesh at every sweep.
Is a cracked crown urgent?
It's rarely an emergency, but it's worth fixing before winter. Every crack lets water into the brick below, and freeze-thaw cycles make the crack and the damage bigger each year. Sealing a crown early is far cheaper than rebuilding the top of a chimney later.
