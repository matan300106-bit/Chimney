Chimneys in Pittsburgh

Old brick, shared walls and a lot of hills

A large share of the city's houses were built before the Second World War, and plenty go back to the late 1800s. That means solid brick chimneys, but also clay-tile liners that have been cracking for decades, flues that were never lined at all, and mortar that has been through a century of freeze-thaw winters.

Many of those fireplaces were built when Pittsburgh heated with coal. Coal fireplaces tend to have small, shallow fireboxes and flues sized for a coal fire, not a modern wood fire. Lots of them have since been closed off, converted to gas logs, or left to vent only the furnace and water heater.

Then there's the city's layout. Rowhouses and twins often share one chimney stack between two homes. Hillside houses can have a short chimney on the uphill side and a tall one on the downhill side. And on a narrow street with a steep roof, just getting a ladder to the chimney takes planning.

Around the city

What we see, neighborhood by neighborhood

Lawrenceville, Bloomfield, South Side

Narrow brick rowhouses and attached homes, many from the late 1800s and early 1900s. Shared stacks, unlined or partly lined flues, and chimneys that have been patched more than once over the years.

Shadyside, Squirrel Hill, Highland Park

Larger older houses, often with two or more fireplaces plus a furnace flue in the same chimney. Tall stacks, slate or tile roofs, and original clay liners that are well past their best.

Mount Washington, Brookline

Hillside lots and steep streets. Downhill chimneys can be very tall, and wind over the ridges can affect draft. Brookline has a lot of early- and mid-century brick homes with gas furnaces venting into the chimney.

Work on the shared crown or the brick above the roof usually affects both houses, so it helps to talk to your neighbor before a repair. We're happy to walk both of you through the photos.
Most-booked in the city

Services Pittsburgh homes need most

Camera inspections

The only way to see the inside of a 100-year-old flue. Essential before you start using an old fireplace again or close on a city house.

Chimney liners

Old clay tiles, or no liner at all, are a poor match for today's gas furnaces and water heaters. A stainless liner gives exhaust a safe, sized path out.

Masonry & tuckpointing

Open joints, spalling brick and leaning stacks above the roofline. We repoint and rebuild to keep old brick standing.

Caps & crowns

A cracked crown lets water straight into the stack. A cap keeps out rain, leaves and the city's pigeons and squirrels.

Pittsburgh FAQ

City chimney questions

More general answers on our FAQ page.

My rowhouse shares a chimney with my neighbor. Can you still work on mine?
Yes. Each house usually has its own flue inside the shared stack, and we sweep and inspect yours. If the crown, cap or brickwork above the roof needs repair, that work covers both flues, so we'll explain what we found and you can decide with your neighbor how to handle it.
Our old fireplace was built for coal. Can we burn wood in it?
Not without an inspection first. Many older city fireplaces were built for coal, with small fireboxes and flues that may be unlined or cracked. We'll check the firebox, damper and flue with a camera and tell you whether it's safe for wood, needs a liner, or is better suited to gas logs or an insert.
Can you inspect the chimney before we close on a house in the city?
Yes. NFPA 211 recommends a Level 2 inspection when a property changes hands, and a general home inspection doesn't look inside the flue. We run a camera through every flue and send a written report you and your agent can use. See our real estate inspection page.
Do you work on steep hillside lots and tall chimneys?
Yes. Steep roofs, tall downhill-side chimneys and tight street access are a normal part of working in Pittsburgh. Let us know about access when you book, so we bring the right ladders and equipment.
